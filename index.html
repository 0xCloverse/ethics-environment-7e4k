Engineers and the Environment

Presenter: 한태영 · Oct. 26, 2026

Readings: Harris et al., Engineering Ethics ch. 9 · Peter Singer, One World ch. 2 "One Atmosphere"

Big question: What responsibility do engineers have for the environment?
Part of the problem

Engineers design plants that pollute, dams that flood farmland, projects that drain wetlands.

Part of the solution

Engineers also design the technology that reduces or removes those threats.

Q1

What is "sustainable development"?

Meet present needs without hurting (1) nature's capacity to absorb our impact, and (2) future generations' ability to meet their own needs. (ASCE)

In short: "Use it, but leave enough for nature and for our grandchildren."

Why it matters

Engineering codes now look beyond today's client to nature and future people. NSPE III.2.d and ASCE both mention it.

Its limit

Most codes say engineers "should strive to" follow it. A recommendation, not a requirement, so it is hard to enforce.

Q2 · Petulla (1987)

Three attitudes toward the environment

Sub-minimal

Less than the law

Do as little as possible. If paying the fine is cheaper than fixing it, pay the fine.

"Break the law if it pays."

Minimalist (compliance)

Exactly the law

Follow regulations, often without enthusiasm. Regulation is just a cost of doing business.

"Only what the law says."

Progressive

Beyond the law

CEO fully supports environmental work. Good for long-term interest, and some are truly committed.

"More than the law asks."

Why go progressive? (1) self-interest: less criticism, fewer lawsuits, new markets · (2) human well-being, including future generations · (3) respect for nature itself

Q3

Anthropocentric vs. non-anthropocentric

Anthropocentric · 인간중심

Only humans have intrinsic value

Nature has instrumental value: it matters because it is useful to us.

Save the forest? For wood, clean air, recreation.

vs
Non-anthropocentric · 비인간중심

Nature has value in itself

At least some natural things have intrinsic value.

Save the forest? Because the forest deserves respect.

Aldo Leopold, "land ethic"

We are members of a "biotic community" with soil, water, plants and animals. Right = preserves its integrity, stability and beauty.

Textbook: a "modified" version

Respect nature for its own sake, but harm can be justified for a large human good. Ex. Austin's blind salamanders vs. saving many human lives.

Q4 · Singer, "One Atmosphere"

What is the Kyoto Protocol? (1997)

Who

39 developed nations got targets.
Developing nations: no targets.

How much

Total cut of at least 5% below 1990 levels by 2012. EU 8%, US 7%.

How

Emissions trading: a country can buy credits from one that has some to spare.

Background: UN Framework Convention on Climate Change (Rio Earth Summit, 1992) → "precautionary principle" and "right to sustainable development".

Criticism

Bush (2000): unfair, China and India were exempt. Lomborg: too costly for a small effect.

Singer's reply

Kyoto is not the solution but the first step to bring developing countries in later.

Q4 · Why is it rather fair?

"You broke it, you fix it"

The atmosphere is one giant shared sink.

For a long time everyone poured waste in, and it seemed endless. Rich countries poured in most of it. Now the sink is nearly full, so those who filled it should clean first.

Singer uses John Locke's idea: you may take only if you leave "enough and as good" for others.

1. Polluter pays

Historical responsibility: whoever emitted the most should cut first.

2. Common but differentiated

Everyone shares the problem, but richer and more responsible nations lead.

3. Right to develop

Poor countries still need to grow out of poverty.

Two examples from the text

Two companies, opposite attitudes

Progressive

3M: "Pollution Prevention Pays" (3P)

  • Since 1975, thousands of 3P projects saved 3M $975 million
  • Early 1990s: $175 million+ of equipment to recycle and burn off solvents
  • Air emissions of volatile organics cut 93% (1990 to 2000), even though plants already met EPA standards

Green and profitable can go together.

Sub-minimal

Shady Chemical and engineer Vivian

  • Its pesticide will soon be banned as a cancer-causing chemical
  • As usual, Shady wants a substitute as close to the old one as possible, just different enough to escape the ban
  • Vivian has good reason to think it is just as dangerous

A clear violation of the "degree-of-harm" criterion: a clear health threat must be removed, and cost is not a major factor.

Case · NSPE Board of Ethical Review, Case 21-2

Must engineers plan for a climate that hasn't arrived yet?

Engineer A designs an access road across a tidal saltmarsh for a developer's health care facility. Local rules only require design based on past weather. No rule mentions sea level rise.

A thinks the project could make 20 upstream homes unlivable 10+ years sooner, and proposes a costly flood study.

Client: "Skip it, unless the regulators ask."

Board's conclusion: A must consider climate impacts on the public, even if the law does not require it.

Step 1Talk to the client: risks, mitigation, and the client's own risk of skipping it
Step 2If refused: put the concern in an engineering report for regulators and the public
Step 3If both refused: withdraw from the project

Code: I.1 public safety paramount · II.3.a no omission of facts · III.2.d sustainable development
