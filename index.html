Q1

What is sustainable development?

Definition (ASCE)

Development that meets present needs without harming nature’s ability to absorb human impact, and without harming future generations’ ability to meet their own needs.

Harris et al., ch. 9

Significance

Engineering codes now consider not only the client, but also nature and future generations (NSPE III.2.d, ASCE).

Limit

The codes only say engineers “should strive to” follow it.
It is a recommendation, not a requirement.

Q2

Three attitudes toward the environment

Sub-minimal

Less than
the law requires

Does as little as possible.
Pays the fine if it is cheaper than fixing the problem.

Minimalist (compliance)

Exactly what
the law requires

Follows regulations, often reluctantly.
Sees them as a cost of doing business.

Progressive

More than
the law requires

Supported by the CEO.
Sees protection as good for the long term, and some firms are genuinely committed.

Reasons for the progressive attitude: self-interest, human well-being, and respect for nature.

Source: Petulla (1987), in Harris et al., ch. 9
Examples

Going beyond the law: Two industry examples

Example 1 · Chemical industry

Responsible Care

The chemical industry’s voluntary pledge to handle chemicals safely
and warn the public of hazards (CMA, US)

  • 1990: 170+ member firms published shared principles
  • Joining is a condition of CMA membership
3M, a member company, went even further

Cut air emissions by 93% (1990 to 2000), even though it already met the legal limits (EPA)

Example 2 · Voluntary principles

CERES Principles

10 environmental rules that companies adopt voluntarily

  • Adopted by oil companies after the Exxon Valdez oil spill (1989, Alaska)
  • Examples:
Protect the biosphereReduce wasteSave energyReduce riskInform the publicAnnual public report

Both go beyond what the law requires. This is the progressive attitude from Q2.

Source: Harris et al., ch. 9 (9.5)
Q3

Anthropocentric vs. Non-anthropocentric

Anthropocentric

Only humans have intrinsic value.

Nature has instrumental value:
it matters because it is useful to people.

e.g. protect a forest for wood, clean air and recreation

Non-anthropocentric

Some natural things have intrinsic value.

Nature matters in itself, not only because people use it.

e.g. protect a forest because it deserves respect

Aldo Leopold: land ethic

Humans are members of a biotic community with soil, water, plants and animals.

Textbook position

A modified non-anthropocentric view: respect nature, but harm can be justified for a large enough human good.
(e.g. blind salamanders in Austin)

CaseNSPE Board of Ethical Review · Case 21-2 (2021)

Must engineers plan for future climate change?

Project

An engineer designs an access road across a tidal saltmarsh for a health care facility.

Regulations

Local rules use past weather data only.
Sea level rise is not considered.

Concern

The project could make 20 upstream homes unlivable 10+ years sooner.
The engineer proposes a flood study.

Client’s response

Skip the study unless regulators ask for it.

Board’s conclusion

The engineer must consider climate impacts on public welfare,
even if the law does not require it.

1

Discuss the risks with the client.

2

If refused, include the concern in an engineering report for regulators and the public.

3

If refused again, withdraw from the project.

NSPE Code I.1, II.3.a, III.2.d

Source: NSPE Board of Ethical Review, Case 21-2
